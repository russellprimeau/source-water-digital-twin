--- 5 ---
Within lentic surface waters, water quality parameters vary spatially and temporally due to complex interactions between natural and artificial processes. These processes are impractical to control and difficult to predict. Transport of contaminants within the boundaries of a surface water body is strongly influenced by hydrodynamics, which precludes simple location-based assessment of the distribution of water quality parameters at the offtake point for a drinking water supply. Timely spatially- and temporally-resolved knowledge of the distribution of water quality parameters can therefore be valuable in operational monitoring, by quantifying the influence of contamination sources on influent (raw) water quality at the plant before contaminants are abstracted. However, present water quality monitoring methods typically provide low resolution and high latency in their estimate of water quality parameters, potentially failing to detect or predict contamination in time to allow effective responses before harm results \citep{adjovu2023overview}. The value of improving methods for detecting and monitoring contamination of surface source water bodies is widely recognized both by industry sources and in academic literature \citep{banna2014online, ngwenya2025monitoring}.

--- 6 ---
A digital twin is a model of a real object or system which combines prior knowledge of the properties of the object and its dynamics with data from networked sensors to create a virtual object which captures the state of the object in real- or near real-time, enabling additional functions. Compared to existing methods for operational monitoring of source water quality in drinking water reservoirs, this previously untested approach offers several potential advantages for monitoring and managing risks related to contaminants which cannot readily be assessed by online methods such as in situ or remote sensing.

--- 7 ---
In this study, the authors investigated the feasibility and utility of applying the framework of digital twins to water quality in a surface source water body by implementing a prototype system on the Brusdalsvatnet drinking water reservoir in Norway. The paper builds on earlier work by the authors described in \citep{primeau2024towards}, which described data collection and an online dashboard. This study instead emphasizes the modeling process and applications. Contributions of the paper include a general framework for developing digital twins of water quality in surface water bodies; presentation of a proof-of-concept implementation of this framework; and demonstration of several proposed applications for such a system, including a nowcast estimation of the current water quality state for operational monitoring, a simulation tool for hazard identification, and a method for optimizing data collection by mobile in situ sensing systems during increased monitoring as part of responding to incidents.

\subsection{Source Water Quality} \label{ch:waterquality} Water quality encompasses physical, chemical and biological parameters describing the constituents of a volume of water. Each water quality parameter is an intensive property which varies throughout any volume or body of water due to a complex interaction between sources, transport processes and reaction processes. The circulation driving transport within lentic water bodies may be complex and time varying under the influence of hydrological factors and meteorological forcing \citep{chapman2021water}. Therefore it is computationally expensive to predict the direction and speed at which transport processes disperse contaminants which affect water quality parameters. Certain chemical and physical parameters can be measured in situ with simple optical and electrochemical devices, but many parameters such as concentrations of so-called "micropollutants" and pathogenic microorganisms require either costly specialized devices or laboratory analysis of samples \citep{zulkifli2018detection,Chidiac2023APerspectives}. This is due in part to the very low concentrations at which such substances can be harmful, on the order of µg/L. Substances which are only harmful at much higher concentrations can more readily be monitored by electrochemical devices.

--- 9 ---
As highlighted in \citep{yang2022review}, remote sensing methods such as satellite imagery are a popular alternative to automate retrieval of water quality data. However, remote sensing is less suitable than in situ methods for monitoring source water quality in many situations. Limitations include trade-offs between the spatial resolution and accuracy \citep{meng2015unified}; susceptibility to interference from ice, clouds and fog \citep{yang2022review}; and limited ability to assess many parameters essential for source water safety. Therefore remote sensing methods have not been applied in this study.

--- 10 ---
Modeling is another alternative to address the sparsity of water quality data, which is most true of parameters which require offline analysis. Instead of collecting more measurements from the environment, data are synthesized from the known input values and relationships between them. In process-driven hydrodynamic water quality models (HWQM) such as those compared in \citep{de2020historical}, a computational fluid dynamics method such as the finite volume method is used to predict the movement of water by numerically approximating solutions to fluid dynamic laws within a discretized volume, coupled to numerical solvers for constitutive equations for other flux and reaction processes. Data-driven surrogate models such as \citep{wang2025spatio} have also been shown to accurately predict spatially distributed water quality parameters. However, per schemas such as \citep{kapoor2024reforms}, data-driven models lack sufficient interpretability and trustworthiness in the absence of more extensive validation data than was available in this study.

--- 11 ---
In the HWQM approach, water quality is quantified in terms of the concentration of individual conservative constituents such as chemical species. As in any numerical model, accurate boundary and initial conditions must be specified. A focus of the digital twin system described in this paper is to integrate data from IoT sensing platforms to define boundary and initial conditions to the model to extrapolate the state of the water body at locations and times where it has not been measured directly.

--- 12 ---
\subsection{Digital Twins} \label{ch:DigitalTwins} The essential feature of a digital twin is a high-fidelity virtual representation of the state of a real object, updated in near real-time using IoT technology where sensor data is transmitted via telecommunications. A digital twin provides robust monitoring of the state of the real object, a desirable capability for the application of protecting water quality. Reviews including \citep{tao2018digital} and \citep{barricelli2019survey} find agreement that in addition to the monitoring function, underpinned by bidirectional, near real-time data exchange between the real and virtual objects, the model component of digital twin systems should provide adaptive, predictive and analytic features. Thus a second defining characteristic of a digital twin is the ability to function as a simulator, using computational methods to calculate future and past states of the system under different conditions. This too has applications in protecting surface water quality, allowing users to anticipate and plan for the progression of different contamination events. Finally, such systems leverage the combination of the volumes of monitoring data and simulated alternatives to provide analysis, serving as a decision support tool for managing the object throughout its lifecycle. The enabling technology of online dashboards provides a practical interface for stakeholders to access the monitoring, simulation, and analysis functions of a digital twin.

--- 13 ---
Digital twins were originally conceived for artificial objects such as manufacturing equipment and the products they produced, but now exist in many domains. As explored in \citep{alam2015design}, the IoT infrastructure requirements can vary widely depending on the type and location of real objects, while still supporting the same monitoring and simulation functions. The functional definition of "real-time" varies between applications based on the dynamics of the modeled system. For monitoring natural environment systems such as surface water, \citep{Murphy2015} offers a practical definition of "near real-time" as latency of one to three hours between observation and data availability. \citep{Zhang2023ATechnologies} presents a digital twin of a research vessel based on co-simulation, with diagnostic monitoring features developed for individual subsystems integrated with a model for simulating the maneuvering and performance of the full ship. \citep{verdouw2021digital} uses the digital twin framework to integrate automation and control across natural, engineered and business systems in the context of operating a farm, including monitoring, control, planning and optimization. Within the water sector digital twins are increasingly recognized as a useful tool for operating built components of water supplies, such as \citep{MA2025108316} for treatment plants and \citep{ConejosFuertes13092020} for distribution networks.

--- 14 ---
Recently, works such as \citep{miedtank2024marine}, \citep{bauer2021digital} and \citep{berre2024iliad} have identified digital twins as a practical approach for monitoring and managing complex natural systems. Strict definitions of digital twin such as in \citep{kritzinger2018digital}, which require control in the form of automatic data flow from the virtual object to the real object, would preclude this framing. Clearly, it is impractical to control the state of many natural systems, such as the temperature of a lake spanning several kilometers. Conversely, there are well-established models of natural systems with data assimilation which meet less restrictive definitions of digital twins. For instance, \citep{blair2021digital} suggests considering operational weather forecasting systems as digital twins of the atmosphere, arguing that it is useful to apply this re-framing to identify possible innovations through knowledge transfer from methods developed for monitoring and control of artificial systems. Digital twins are therefore understood to include systems which lack automated control of the real object within this paper, in keeping with the usage established by these works.

--- 15 ---
Research on this topic is nascent, with several ongoing projects having published preliminary results. For instance, \citep{vasilijevic2024digital} presents a system for monitoring and simulating numerous parameters representing the state of water in a coastal area. \citep{xu2024design} presents a twin of an urban river system, incorporating a variety of data sources, and \citep{yao2025feature} presents a system with similar applications in predicting water quality in an urban river system from real-time sensors. To the authors' knowledge, there are no extant examples of digital twins of surface drinking water reservoirs for the purpose of monitoring or protecting drinking water quality.

\subsection{Hydrodynamic Water Quality Model}

--- 23 ---
Before operating the model online in a digital twin, an offline calibration process is used to ensure model constants and processes accurately reproduce historical data. This process is iterative and recursive: first calibrating hydrological processes to ensure accurate volume balance; then calibrating heat transfer processes to ensure accurate thermal balance, and re-balancing hydrological processes to account for e.g. changes to volume losses through evaporation; then calibrating chemical and/or biological flux processes to ensure balance, from available samples and time series. There are design tradeoffs between the data available, the parameters modeled, and model certainty.

--- 25 ---
The process models module in Figure~\ref{fig:IO} consists of an off-the-shelf HWQM solver which numerically solves hydrodynamic equations for movement of water and coupled advection-diffusion-reaction equations for concentration of each of $N$ defined water quality constituents $n,n+1,...N$. The solution method presented here is adapted from \citep{D-WAQ_manual}. The framework can be adapted to any system of substances and processes for which constitutive differential equations are known. For a 3D finite volume model using a mesh with parallel vertical layers on Cartesian grid coordinates, the advection-diffusion-reaction equation can be expressed as Equation~\ref{Eq:advection-diffusion}:

--- 26 ---
\begin{multline} \label{Eq:advection-diffusion} \frac{\partial C_n}{\partial t}=D_x \frac{\partial^2 C_n}{\partial x^2}-u \frac{\partial C_n}{\partial x}+D_y \frac{\partial^2 C_n}{\partial y^2}-v \frac{\partial C_n}{\partial y} +D_z \frac{\partial^2 C_n}{\partial z^2}-w \frac{\partial C_n}{\partial z}+\sum S(t,...) \end{multline}

--- 27 ---
\noindent where $C_n$ is concentration of the constituent $n$, with dimensions of mass per volume; $t$ is time; $D_x, D_y, D_z$ are the dispersion coefficients (from Fick's diffusion law) for the constituent (typically $D_x=D_y$); $u$, $v$, $w$ are the components of the fluid's velocity vector; and $S(t,...)$ is each source or sink of constituent $n$ within the control volume, such as inflows and reaction processes. Each $S$ term can be a function of time and state variables such as temperature, pressure, and the concentration of all constituents. Heat transfer is also solved using Equation~\ref{Eq:advection-diffusion}, by altering the dimensions of the concentration variables to describe temperature and energy.

--- 28 ---
\begin{equation} \label{Eq:continuity} \frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=\int_0^h\left(q_{\text {in }}-q_{\text {out }}\right) d z+P_{recip}-E_{vap}+\frac{\partial \bar{u} h}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial \bar{v} h}{\partial y} \end{equation}

--- 29 ---
\noindent where $\rho$ is mass density; $P$ is pressure; $f$ is the Coriolis parameter, representing the apparent force due to the Earth's rotation at a particular latitude; $\nu_V$ is the vertical eddy viscosity coefficient; $F_x$, $F_y$ are Reynolds stresses with dimensions of force; $M_x$, $M_y$ are external sources and sinks of momentum, such as interactions with structures, inflows or outflows.

Equations \ref{Eq:advection-diffusion}--\ref{Eq:hydrostatic} are sufficient to describe conservation of energy, mass and momentum within the model volume, but additional terms are needed to represent boundary conditions at the free surface, bed, and shoreline. Shear stresses from wind and bed friction, wave dynamics, heat transfer from various processes, and mass and momentum transfer from inflows and outflows including precipitation and evaporation provide functions which force the hydrodynamics, and thus the concentrations of water quality constituents.

--- 35 ---
In principle it is possible to use water quality data from the profiler and USV to define additional boundary conditions or refine the initial conditions, fixing the value for these parameters in the 3D grid cells where they are measured. However, such an approach risks over-defining the model. More importantly, using these observations to constrain the model would reduce the amount of independent ground-truth data available for validation. Profiler observations are used to define the initial temperature field; subsequent profiler observations are withheld from model forcing and used for validation and model-selection/error-reduction procedures.

--- 36 ---
Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow} contextualizes this model solution process within an update loop incorporating other components of the digital twin system. The sensing platforms described in Section~\ref{ch:sensing} periodically provide updated boundary conditions, prompting a model run to update the nowcast, or estimated current state of the lake, in terms of select water quality parameters. This estimate is compared against the most recent in situ water quality measurements from the profiler and/or USV. To account for uncertainty in input variables, an ensemble of parallel model estimates can be computed within each model update interval to create a distribution of outputs. The model update is run hourly to match the sample rate of the weather station and profiler surface series, and takes up to an hour to compute all ensemble members. Thus the latency between the period during which the profiler measures water quality parameters and the online estimate of water quality is under two hours, meeting the near real-time definition proposed in \citep{Murphy2015}. This is far less latency than methods for directly measuring water quality parameter values at all points throughout the depth and span of the lake.

--- 37 ---
The accuracy of these alternative outputs is compared to the validation dataset from in situ sensors, and the most accurate model output is used as the best estimate of the lake's current state as well as the initial condition for the subsequent model update, as indicated in Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow}. Sensitivity analysis for the altered input parameters can be conducted by comparing the solver outputs of alternative model configurations. As described in Section~\ref{ch:pathplan}, the sensitivity analysis can be used to optimize data collection plans for the USV during emergency responses, as indicated in Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow}. Analysis of scenarios such as acute contaminant releases does not take place within the operational model loop, but leverages past and present sensor data and state estimates to simulate realistic conditions.

--- 38 ---
The model scripts are managed and run in a Windows environment described in Table~\ref{tab:computer}, with Windows Task Scheduler and Python scripting used for automation. A private application developed in Python provides supervisory control for functions such as adjusting baseline modeling assumptions, adjusting automation tasks and schedules, and performing advanced analyses which require larger computational resources. A public interface for a limited set of the monitoring, simulation and analysis functions is provided by an online dashboard, described in the earlier work \citep{primeau2024towards}.

--- 39 ---
An automated workflow retrieves data from servers maintained by the profiler and weather station manufacturers and accumulates them within local files. The retrieval rate matches the update rate of the data in the manufacturers' servers: hourly for the surface profiler data and weather attributes, and twice a day for the vertical profiles.

--- 40 ---
To be trusted as a decision support tool, the model must demonstrate skill in predicting the ground truth state of the real object. By providing a near real-time assessment of water quality conditions which may indicate the failure of control measures in the watershed through the online dashboard, the nowcast assists in the water safety planning task of operational monitoring. Because of the large number of model input parameters, an iterative process of simulation and parameter tuning is necessary to attain a configuration with acceptable skill. Configuration parameters include the spatial and temporal resolution, physical process parameters, and solver method parameters, which each affect the computational cost and accuracy of model outputs. In each iteration, a period with continuous forcing data from sensors is simulated and evaluated according to how closely the predictions agree with values observed at the profiler station, for certain indicative parameters. The performance of the different model configurations for these parameters can be compared using error norms for each time series output by the model with equivalent sensor data, including root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient. Once the calibrated model is configured, it can be updated from new sensor data at regular intervals to provide a 3D estimate of the state of water quality parameters for monitoring purposes.

--- 41 ---
It is beneficial to calibrate the model configuration using the longest complete time span for which validation data are available. Adjusting model configurations based on errors computed from short simulation spans risks overfitting the model to that span. The relative magnitude of different processes and parameters varies seasonally, so inaccuracies in the model of one or more processes may not be apparent within short spans. During spring and early summer there is significant net heat transfer into the lake. Inaccuracies in the rate of heat loss due to processes such as outgoing longwave radiation may be concealed by minor adjustments to the incoming heat fluxes. Such an error only becomes apparent when the simulation includes the fall season, during which heat loss processes are of greater magnitude than incoming heat flux. The same risk of overfitting exists if the model configuration is adjusted continuously during an online operational simulation, which is why the model configuration must be tuned offline.

--- 42 ---
In this study, the Delft3D FM suite of modeling tools by Deltares was selected for reasons including its open source code and free licensing; active development; and past validation studies such as \citep{Delft_Validation1} demonstrating its effectiveness in the setting of cold, seasonally frozen lakes. Delft3D FM uses a custom matrix solver algorithm based on a conjugate gradient method with Gaussian elimination and other adaptations to efficiently solve Equations~\ref{Eq:advection-diffusion}--\ref{Eq:hydrostatic}. One limitation of the software is that it does not model solid-liquid phase transitions. Calibrated online modeling is therefore only possible from approximately April to November because of freeze-thaw dynamics in the study area during winter months. Winter operation is also constrained by the practical difficulty of automated water-quality measurement under ice, which limits the availability of forcing and validation data.

--- 43 ---
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow}, the function of in situ data within the model loop is to validate model accuracy and refine model accuracy by adjusting inputs. However, the fixed $x,y$ position of the profiler limits its sensitivity to some model inputs, such as distant inflows. Thus the value of the USV's additional DOFs lies in collecting validation data from points other than the profiler, which are more sensitive to other model inputs. Data collected at points where the output parameter has low sensitivity to the model inputs will be less informative as to the accuracy of those inputs. Because the USV is constrained by limited battery range and other factors, it is preferable to collect data only at the points which provide the most informative dataset.

--- 44 ---
To compare the sensitivity of different model locations, alternative simulation outputs must be generated by varying one or more model inputs. Differences between the output created from these model configuration alternatives can be analyzed to identify the locations with the greatest variance in the final timestep, and thus the greatest sensitivity to the varied input. In Equation~\ref{eq:stdev}, the sensitivity of the model's estimate of the value of an output parameter $P$ (such as contaminant concentration) for each 2D cell $i$ is calculated as the standard deviation $\sigma_i$ of the depth-averaged value of that parameter across $M$ alternative model output sets which are calculated using divergent input assumptions (such as alternative model configuration parameters or forcing datasets). The output parameter $P$ is depth-averaged within each 2D cell.

--- 45 ---
\begin{equation} \sigma_i = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} \left(P_i^{(m)} - \mu_i\right)^2} \label{eq:stdev} \end{equation}

--- 46 ---
Taking $S = \{ \sigma_i \mid i \in I \}$ as the set of the sensitivity values for $I$ total 2D cells and normalizing the values as $\sigma_i' = \frac{\sigma_i}{\max(S)}$ to avoid computational artifacts, the indices of the subset $R$ of the $r$ 2D cells with the highest sensitivity values are identified in Equation~\ref{eq:top}. The indices in $R$ are used to look up the coordinates of the centroid of each of these $r$ most sensitive cells.

--- 47 ---
\begin{equation} R = \argmax_{\substack{R \subset \{1, \dots, I\}, \\ |R| = r}} \sum_{i \in R} \sigma_i \label{eq:top} \end{equation}

--- 48 ---
To avoid redundant measurements within a single area of high sensitivity, a DBSCAN clustering analysis identifies coordinates which are within a threshold distance of one another, and returns a shorter set of coordinates $C$ consisting of the centroids of these clusters. Each cluster $c_1,...c_K$ is assigned a weight $w_1,...w_K$ equal to the sum of the sensitivity values of the cells within the cluster:

--- 49 ---
\begin{equation} w_k = \sum_{j \in c_k} \sigma_j, \quad \text{for } k = 1, \dots, K \label{eq:cluster} \end{equation}

--- 50 ---
Finally, given a start and end point from which the USV can be launched and recovered, a brute-force search method identifies the ordered subset $C^*$ which achieves the highest sum of weights $W_{k}$, as presented in Equation~\ref{eq:optimizer1} where $d(\mathbf{c}_{i}, \mathbf{c}_{i+1})$ is the distance between consecutive points in the subset. This path is subject to a maximum path length constraint $D_{max}$ which represents operational considerations such as the USV's battery capacity.

--- 51 ---
\begin{equation} C^* = \argmax_{\substack{C' \subseteq C \\ \sum d(\mathbf{c}_{i}, \mathbf{c}_{i+1}) \leq D_{\max}}} \sum_{\mathbf{c}_{i} \in C'} W_{i} \label{eq:optimizer1} \end{equation}

--- 52 ---
The case study integrated the monitoring equipment and models according to the framework described in Section~\ref{ch:Methodology} to create a twin of the Brusdalsvatnet reservoir located in Ålesund Municipality, Norway. This dimictic, oligotrophic enlarged natural lake is the main drinking water source for two municipalities with a combined population of nearly 70,000. The single horizontally-well-mixed main basin with strong seasonal stratification makes calibrating to all depths from a single horizontal point relatively reliable. Basic physical characteristics of the lake are presented in Table~\ref{tab:Brusdalsvatnet}.

--- 53 ---
Physical features of Brusdalsvatnet and its catchment area are mapped in Figure~\ref{fig:map}, including hydrology and measuring points for use in the case study. Neither the principal inflows nor outflows to the lake are continuously metered, though a limited dataset of outflow and surface elevation was available for calibration. The dataset cannot be shared publicly due to ownership and safety restrictions, so the hydrological calibration is omitted here. The various seasonal and permanent inflows to Brusdalsvatnet were represented as eight discrete tributaries, with constant flows from \citep{mohammed2019impact} used to approximate annual average discharge in their vicinity.

--- 54 ---
Previous assessment has identified several specific hazards to Brusdalsvatnet's water quality. Historically, non-point pollution from stormwater runoff has carried microbial contaminants from animal activity within the watershed into the lake. Roadway expansion and traffic growth have been linked to heavy metal contamination. Point sources of pollution such as leakages from aging wastewater infrastructure in the urbanized western end of the watershed have been linked to increased E. Coli concentrations in \citep{mohammed2019impact}. Conversely, the limited nutrient inflows and high latitude provide strong inherent protection against eutrophication, limiting the need to measure chlorophyll-A or other related parameters.

The results of each iterative simulation in a parameter tuning and mesh convergence study are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:meshconverge}. On the y-axis is the primary error norm used to compare model configurations, the mean depth-wise RMSE. The model temperature at each model layer and timestep is compared against time-series data from the profiler station, and the RMSE values from the depth contours are averaged. Pearson correlation coefficient (depicted using color-coding in Figure~\ref{fig:meshconverge}) and other error norms such as mean absolute error are used to detect other undesirable behaviors, since averages can conceal differences between the model and ground truth datasets (e.g. Anscombe's quartet). The real computation time per simulation time is used as a metric of computational expense.

--- 59 ---
Importantly, the simulation runs at a high multiple of the real process time, enabling multiple simulation iterations within a single update interval. Criteria selected for model validation were that 1) RMSE in depth-averaged temperature must be below 1$^{\circ}$C across a full season, 2) Pearson correlation coefficient of greater than 0.95, and 3) the relationship between model complexity and computational cost must converge to a discernible asymptote, such that increasing the computational cost of the model (e.g. by increasing mesh resolution from 9,884 cells to 65,904 cells as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:meshconverge}) would yield negligible accuracy improvements compared to other sources of error. These criteria are achieved in the three model configurations indicated in blue in Figure~\ref{fig:meshconverge}, where increasing computational cost by refining temporal resolution yields negligible improvements in accuracy. The lowest-error configuration was therefore selected for use in the monitoring application, providing users with an estimated value for parameters such as temperature and velocity at all locations and times from model initialization through the latest interval for which forcing data is provided, rather than only those locations and time from which ground-truth measurements are available.

--- 60 ---
\subsection{Hazard Assessment} \label{ch:contamination} Once the model is calibrated, it can also be used offline as a simulation tool for analyzing potential hazards as part of the system assessment and management planning tasks within water safety planning. While the model does not assist in identifying the hazards themselves, it can be used to assess the risk and consequence of different incidents within the watershed in terms of the impact on water quality at the offtake. To demonstrate this capability, a real contingency scenario involving possible release of chemical contaminants from construction activities was simulated. The simulation evaluates contaminant concentrations at selected locations following the release.

--- 61 ---
\begin{figure}[t] \centering \includegraphics[width=0.99\linewidth]{Fig7.png} \caption{Modeled concentration of ammonium in gN/m$^3$ 12 hours post-release (\textbf{a}) at the surface, (\textbf{b}) at 6.25~m depth, and (\textbf{c}) along an x-z cross section.\label{fig:contam}} \end{figure}

--- 62 ---
Based on the analysis of a consultant involved in planning the construction, the worst-case scenario for the release is modeled as 3.5 kg of two water-soluble nitrogen-rich ionic compounds found in explosives, ammonium (NH$_4^+$) and nitrate (NO$_3^-$), in a 1:1 ratio. At the concentrations which are of concern for human consumption, these contaminants are in fact possible to detect directly using sensors and therefore not an example of the sort of micropollutant for which the water quality digital twin application is most suited. However, given the lack of such sensors in the case study, they are used as the model substances here to demonstrate how inferring distribution from sparse measurements or assumptions can still provide useful insight.

--- 63 ---
Both nitrogen compounds are highly chemically reactive and thus in addition to transport, concentrations are affected by reaction processes. Nitrification and denitrification reaction process models are included in the simulation scenario presented here. The basic chemical formulas for nitrification and denitrification are presented in Equation~\ref{eq:nitrification} and Equation~\ref{eq:denitrification}. These equations are represented in the reaction process model as a set of partial differential equations including terms for temperature and dissolved oxygen, the solutions of which are $S$ terms in Equation~\ref{Eq:advection-diffusion} for the respective chemical species.

--- 64 ---

--- 65 ---
To provide boundary condition forcing data for a time window that extends beyond the horizon of weather forecasts, seasonally appropriate historical weather data from the digital twin's database of sensor readings were combined with the available extent of data from the 2024 season. The season was simulated from spring turnover to the time of contaminant release to provide realistic initial conditions.

--- 66 ---
The simulated dispersal of the contaminant plume 12 hours after release is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:contam}. Figure~\ref{fig:contam}a shows concentrations in the surface layer, which remain highest at the blast site. Compared to Figure~\ref{fig:contam}b, which shows the layer centered at 6.25~m depth on the western end of the lake, it is apparent that despite the initial release at the surface layer in shallow stratified waters, the highest concentration occurs below the surface. The cross section in the xz plane in Figure~\ref{fig:contam}c, taken along the line at longitude 6.399$^{\circ}$ E shown in Figure~\ref{fig:contam}b, demonstrates that the combined transport processes disperse the plume across the entire width of the lake. This example highlights the value of representing three-dimensional dispersion, which could be poorly resolved by lower-resolution models or missed by sparse point measurements. In this case, there are implications for planning interventions. The simulation suggests that surface-only containment measures, such as floating booms, may be insufficient.

--- 67 ---
Analysis of the contamination scenario can be used to determine how best to allocate limited resources to protect drinking water quality during the construction process. If predicted maximum concentrations remain within acceptable limits under conservative assumptions after accounting for model uncertainty, mitigation measures may not be warranted. Conversely, if the levels are unacceptable, the results can be used to plan and compare mitigation measures such as alterations to the blasting plans or placement of protective curtains in the water to maximize containment of the anticipated plume.

\subsection{Optimized Data Collection Plan}

--- 70 ---
Increased monitoring is a recommended component of incident response plans for identified hazards. In addition to the nitrogen process models described in Section~\ref{ch:contamination}, simulations with two alternative reaction models were conducted for the same time period: a conservative tracer with no reaction, and a non-conservative tracer with a fixed decay rate. This resulted in three unique sets of predictions for the final distributions of ammonium and nitrate concentration three months (92 days) after the contamination event. The alternative predictions for ammonium concentration are used to demonstrate the data collection planning method based on sensitivity analysis described in Section~\ref{ch:pathplan}. The standard deviation of the depth-averaged ammonium concentration in each 2D cell from the population of the three alternative predictions at the final timestep is represented in the heat map shown in Figure~\ref{fig:planner}a. In principle, any set of alternative model configurations can be used to assess sensitivity. For instance, weather forcing data from previous years could be inserted, or the value of any individual solver parameter could be adjusted.

--- 71 ---
The individual 2D cells are ranked in terms of uncertainty in the dependent variable, ammonium concentration. The 50 cells with the greatest uncertainty are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:planner}b. To avoid redundant measurements from closely-spaced locations, clustering analysis identified six clusters A-F based on a threshold for separation of 100~m, indicated using the color coding for the 50 cells plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:planner}b. The centroid of each cluster serves as a surrogate measuring point for the cluster. Each waypoint is assigned a weight based on the summed sensitivity of each point in the cluster. An optimizer returns the data collection plan for which 1) the sum of the weights of clusters visited is maximized, 2) the total path length is minimized, and 3) the maximum path length is not exceeded. Though Cluster B has the highest individual weight, any plan which includes this point exceeds a constraint on maximum path length, in this case set to 6 km. The optimized USV path plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:planner}b beginning and ending at the launch-and-recovery point (1) and with measurement waypoints at the centroids of Clusters A (2), E (3), C (4) and D (5) represents the highest-scoring, shortest path, subject to the maximum distance constraint.

--- 72 ---
This data collection plan is exported as a mission file which the USV can execute. The mission is uploaded to the USV, which must be manually launched and recovered before uploading the collected data to the digital twin's database. As shown in the model update loop in Figure~\ref{fig:dataflow}, the data from the USV mission are used to calculate a new error norm from each alternative model which made predictions for times compatible with the time at which the data was collected, to compare the accuracy of the alternative models.

\section{Discussion} \label{ch:Discussion} The boundaries of the digital twin as described here only include components responsible for operational monitoring, and not the control measures which exercise direct influence over influent water quality or treated water quality. Within water safety plans, preventative and remedial measures require a broader set of resources such as the staff and equipment of the water supplier, including a safely planned and operated treatment and distribution system. Yet by improving the efficiency and accuracy of operational monitoring and hazard response planning in source waters, the analysis and information provided by the digital twin contribute directly to the efficacy of the water safety plan and reduce demands on these downstream components of the water supply.

--- 75 ---
Integrating simulation and analysis with the monitoring system both improves the efficiency of existing functions and enables new capabilities. One such efficiency is referenced in Section~\ref{ch:contamination}: seasonally-appropriate historical data is readily available to force simulations for future periods, without the need for reformatting or computation.

--- 76 ---
One practical difficulty encountered in implementing the prototype was the prevalence of gaps and unreliable values in the sensor datasets. While gaps in the temperature dataset used as validation data only reduce confidence in the accuracy of the model output, gaps in the forcing data must be backfilled from alternative sources, synthetic data or generic assumptions about conditions such as linear interpolation. As more data is accumulated over time, it will be possible to implement more sophisticated approximations of the values of missing data accounting for seasonality and other trends.

--- 77 ---
Another approach would be to model additional processes with outputs corresponding to the parameters measured by the in situ sensors, such as pH, dissolved oxygen, and turbidity. This would allow for a more robust multi-parameter validation process, which would be less susceptible to problems with individual sensors. Likewise, additional sensing systems for parameters and contaminants predicted by the model could be added to the lake.

--- 78 ---
The path planning algorithm presented in Section~\ref{ch:pathplan} in Figure~\ref{fig:planner} makes several simplifying assumptions, and lacks experimental validation with comparison against alternative data collection planning methods. It represents only a first step toward a systematic approach to utilizing the 4DOF of the USV as a sensing platform. A more rigorous stochastic approach such as Monte Carlo simulation or a Gaussian process could be used to quantify and address uncertainty in the model from sources such as measurement error, as partially quantified in Appendix~\ref{app:sensorspecs}.

\section{Conclusions} \label{ch:Conclusions}

--- 80 ---
The study demonstrates that integrating IoT monitoring with a calibrated hydrodynamic model can provide near-real-time spatial estimates beyond directly measured locations. The case study validates the hydrodynamic nowcast against profiler observations and demonstrates scenario analysis and model-informed mobile sampling as additional decision-support applications. Further work is required to validate modeled chemical/biological variables and establish whether the proposed sampling strategy improves information gain relative to alternative approaches.

--- 81 ---
The prototype system is adapted for the threats, needs and available data particular to the target watershed, but the methods may be generalized to other environments based on the available background knowledge on the water supply, such as identified hazards and specific contaminants, watershed size, bathymetric data, and lake dynamics. For instance, controlled reservoirs with more substantial year-round changes in storage would almost certainly need to account for erosion and sediment transport in the draw-down zone. As noted, while not a major concern in the deep, cold, northerly lake used for the case study, nutrient-driven eutrophication and other photosynthesis-driven factors such as aquatic plant growth would be key biochemical processes to model in surface waters for much of the world.

--- 82 ---
This relationship works in both directions: the system demonstrates the capacity to leverage historic data to simulate and analyze past and hypothetical events. With integrated functions for analyzing the output of the baseline and alternative simulations, including feedback for optimizing use of the sensing systems, the water quality digital twin also provides water quality managers with novel capabilities for assessing and managing threats to water quality, which can in turn drive the design in terms of the sensors and process models to include.

--- 83 ---
Water suppliers seeking to implement a version of the system presented here would need to address the financial barriers to deploying suitable hardware and software components. A more complex barrier to adoption is institutional capacity across disciplines such as configuring networked sensors, numerical modeling, and data science. Globally, lack of technical capacity is often a barrier to digital transformation efforts by water suppliers, especially in community-managed or other low-resource settings, including developing countries \citep{daniel2023survey}. Wider adoption could be simplified by standardizing modular components such as the sensing systems and dashboard software, but will still require considerable adaptation to the conditions and hazards unique to each particular water supply, in keeping with the overall principles of water safety plans. Similarly, standardized sources for background and forcing data such as public global weather forecast and reanalysis datasets may be used to reduce the need for on-site data collection, at the cost of reduced ability to adapt to unique local conditions.

--- 84 ---
Expanding the boundaries of a water quality digital twin system to monitor contamination hazards further upstream in the catchment area and further downstream within the treatment and distribution systems is a clear direction for future research. User experience is another direction for investigation: with appropriate assessment of stakeholder needs, the rudimentary applications demonstrated here could be developed toward a more integrated decision support system for source water protection. Beyond the single case study demonstrated here, the water quality digital twin architecture could be adapted to other water bodies to confirm its generality, given appropriate sources of sensor data and background assumptions about static properties such as bathymetry. Surface water quality in both freshwater and seawater has relevance for many other applications beyond drinking water, including food production and ecosystem services.
